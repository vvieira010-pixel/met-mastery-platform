## How to use this bank

This collection contains twelve B2 modules and twelve B2+ modules. Each module includes a learning objective, an explanation, examples, a common mistake, a mini-check, an original MET-style application, an answer key or model response, and feedback criteria. The explanations and activities are written entirely in English so that they can be used directly in an English-medium learning platform.

# Part I — B2 Materials

## B2 Material 1 — Modals for deduction and probability

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Grammar  
**Topic:** Making logical guesses  
**Prerequisite:** Basic modal verbs  
**Suggested time:** 12 minutes  
**Tags:** modals, deduction, probability, must, might, can’t

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to use modal verbs to express different degrees of certainty about a present situation.

### Explanation

Use **must** when the evidence makes you strongly believe something is true: *The lights are on, so someone must be home.* Use **might**, **may**, or **could** when something is possible but not certain: *The package might arrive today.* Use **can’t** when the evidence makes something seem impossible: *That can’t be the right address; the building number is different.*

For deductions about the past, use **must have + past participle**, **might have + past participle**, or **can’t have + past participle**: *She must have forgotten the meeting*; *They might have taken a different route*; *He can’t have seen the message.*

The modal shows the speaker’s judgment, not a guaranteed fact. In formal writing, use these forms carefully and support them with evidence.

### Examples

- The office is empty. The staff **may be** at lunch.
- You have been working all night. You **must be** tired.
- He left ten minutes ago. He **can’t have arrived** yet.
- The road is wet. It **might have rained** earlier.

### Common mistake

Do not use **to** after a modal: *She must to be busy* is incorrect. Say **She must be busy**. Also, do not confuse certainty with possibility: *must* is much stronger than *might*.

### Mini-check

Choose the best answer.

1. The door is locked and all the lights are off. They ___ be inside.  
(A) must (B) can’t (C) should

2. I cannot find Alex. He ___ have gone home.  
(A) might (B) can’t (C) mustn’t

3. The report is on her desk. She ___ have received it.  
(A) must (B) can’t (C) would

**Answer key:** 1-B; 2-A; 3-A.

**Feedback:** The evidence makes presence unlikely in item 1, possible in item 2, and highly likely in item 3.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Reading  
**Instruction:** Read the situation and choose the best inference.

The meeting room is empty, but several notebooks and half-full cups are still on the table. The projector is warm.

What probably happened?

(A) The room has never been used.  
(B) A meeting may have ended recently.  
(C) The projector cannot be switched on.  
(D) The notebooks must belong to the cleaner.

**Model answer:** B.

**Feedback criteria:** The answer should connect the physical evidence to a reasonable inference. “May have ended recently” expresses probability without claiming certainty.

## B2 Material 2 — Conditionals for real and hypothetical situations

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Grammar  
**Topic:** Conditions and consequences  
**Prerequisite:** Present and past verb forms  
**Suggested time:** 14 minutes  
**Tags:** conditionals, if clauses, hypothetical situations

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to distinguish real future conditions from hypothetical present and past conditions.

### Explanation

Use the **first conditional** for a possible future condition and its likely result: **If + present simple, will + verb**. *If the weather improves, we will continue the event.*

Use the **second conditional** for an unlikely or imaginary present or future situation: **If + past simple, would + verb**. *If I had more time, I would volunteer every week.*

Use the **third conditional** for an unreal past situation and an imagined past result: **If + past perfect, would have + past participle**. *If they had checked the address, they would not have taken the wrong bus.*

The result clause can come first: *We will continue if the weather improves.* A comma is normally used when the if-clause comes first.

### Examples

- If you finish early, you **can join** us.
- If I lived closer, I **would attend** more events.
- If we had left earlier, we **would have caught** the train.

### Common mistake

Do not use **would** directly after *if* in standard conditional sentences: *If I would have known* is generally incorrect for this meaning. Use **If I had known, I would have...**

### Mini-check

Complete each sentence.

1. If the library is open, I ___ there after work. (go / will go)
2. If I ___ more confident, I would apply for the position. (am / were)
3. If she had read the instructions, she ___ the mistake. (would avoid / would have avoided)

**Answer key:** 1. will go; 2. were; 3. would have avoided.

**Feedback:** The first sentence describes a possible future. The second imagines a different present. The third imagines a different past and therefore needs the third conditional.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Write three sentences about improving a community event. Use one first conditional, one second conditional, and one third conditional.

**Model response:** “If more volunteers register, the event will run more smoothly. If I were the organizer, I would create a clearer schedule. If the team had advertised earlier, more people would have attended.”

**Feedback criteria:** Check whether each conditional expresses the intended time and reality. The response should use accurate verb forms and a logical relationship between condition and result.

## B2 Material 3 — Reported speech and reporting verbs

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Grammar  
**Topic:** Reporting information accurately  
**Prerequisite:** Past simple and present perfect  
**Suggested time:** 14 minutes  
**Tags:** reported speech, reporting verbs, tense shift

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to report statements, questions, and requests while maintaining the original meaning.

### Explanation

When reporting a past statement, the tense often moves one step back. Present simple may become past simple: *“I need help” → She said that she needed help.* Present perfect may become past perfect: *“I have finished” → He said that he had finished.* **Will** may become **would**, and **can** may become **could**.

Time and place expressions may also change: *today → that day*, *tomorrow → the next day*, *here → there*. However, a tense shift is not always necessary when the information is still true: *The guide said that the museum is open on Sundays.*

For reported questions, use statement word order: *She asked where I lived*, not *She asked where did I live*. For requests, use **asked + object + to + verb**: *He asked me to wait.*

### Examples

- “We are leaving soon.” → They said they **were leaving** soon.
- “Have you seen the notice?” → She asked whether I **had seen** the notice.
- “Please send the form.” → The clerk asked me **to send** the form.

### Common mistake

Do not keep question word order after a reporting verb. *He asked what was the problem* is correct; *He asked what was the problem?* may be punctuated as a question in direct speech, but reported questions use normal statement order.

### Mini-check

Choose the correct reported version.

1. “I can attend on Friday.”  
(A) She said she could attend on Friday.  
(B) She said she can attended on Friday.

2. “Where did you park?”  
(A) He asked where I had parked.  
(B) He asked where had I parked.

3. “Please close the window.”  
(A) She asked me close the window.  
(B) She asked me to close the window.

**Answer key:** 1-A; 2-A; 3-B.

**Feedback:** Reported questions use statement order, and reported requests require an object plus the infinitive with *to*.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Reading  
**Instruction:** Choose the sentence that accurately reports the announcement.

The coordinator said, “The workshop will begin at ten, but participants should arrive early.”

(A) The coordinator said that the workshop had begun at ten and participants arrived late.  
(B) The coordinator said that the workshop would begin at ten, but participants should arrive early.  
(C) The coordinator asked whether the workshop began at ten.  
(D) The coordinator denied that participants needed to arrive early.

**Model answer:** B.

**Feedback criteria:** The answer must preserve both the future time reference and the advice about arriving early.

## B2 Material 4 — Relative clauses and sentence expansion

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Grammar  
**Topic:** Adding precise information  
**Prerequisite:** Sentence subjects and objects  
**Suggested time:** 12 minutes  
**Tags:** relative clauses, defining, non-defining, who, which, that

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to combine ideas with relative clauses and choose appropriate relative pronouns.

### Explanation

A relative clause gives more information about a noun. Use **who** for people, **which** for things, and **that** for people or things in many defining clauses. A defining relative clause identifies exactly which person or thing we mean: *The book that you recommended was useful.* No commas are used.

A non-defining relative clause adds extra information about an already identified noun. Use **who** or **which**, and separate the clause with commas: *The library, which opened last year, is already popular.* Do not use **that** in a non-defining clause.

The relative pronoun can sometimes be omitted when it is the object: *The film that we watched was excellent* → *The film we watched was excellent.* It cannot be omitted when it is the subject: *The woman who organized the event thanked us.*

### Examples

- The student **who won the prize** studies engineering.
- The room **that we reserved** is on the second floor.
- The park, **which is near the station**, is easy to reach.

### Common mistake

Do not use commas around a defining clause if the information identifies the noun. Compare: *The employees who work remotely need access* with *The employees, who work remotely, need access.* The second sentence suggests that all the employees work remotely.

### Mini-check

Choose the best option.

1. The volunteer ___ showed us the entrance was very helpful.  
(A) who (B) which

2. The new center, ___ opened in June, offers evening classes.  
(A) that (B) which

3. The report ___ I sent is attached.  
(A) that (B) who

**Answer key:** 1-A; 2-B; 3-A.

**Feedback:** Use *who* for a person, *which* after a non-defining comma clause, and *that* for a thing in a defining clause.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Combine each pair of sentences into one sentence using a relative clause.

The city opened a study center. The study center offers free internet access.  
The coordinator is an experienced teacher. She designed the program.

**Model response:** “The city opened a study center that offers free internet access. The coordinator, who is an experienced teacher, designed the program.”

**Feedback criteria:** Check accurate pronoun choice, clause placement, punctuation, and preservation of the original meaning.

## B2 Material 5 — Academic and professional collocations

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Vocabulary  
**Topic:** Natural word combinations  
**Prerequisite:** General academic vocabulary  
**Suggested time:** 12 minutes  
**Tags:** collocations, academic English, professional English

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to use common collocations for discussing evidence, decisions, problems, and results.

### Explanation

Collocations are words that commonly occur together. They make writing and speaking more precise and natural. Learn the entire combination rather than translating each word separately.

| Function | Useful collocations |
|---|---|
| Decisions | make a decision, reach an agreement, set a goal |
| Evidence | gather evidence, provide evidence, support a claim |
| Problems | address an issue, solve a problem, raise a concern |
| Results | achieve a result, have an impact, draw a conclusion |
| Communication | express an opinion, clarify a point, deliver a message |

Some collocations take a particular preposition: **contribute to**, **depend on**, **result in**, **focus on**, and **be responsible for**.

### Examples

- The survey **provides evidence** that students need more practice.
- The team **reached an agreement** after a long discussion.
- The policy may **have an impact on** local businesses.

### Common mistake

Avoid combinations based only on literal translation. Say **make a decision**, not *do a decision*; **strong evidence**, not *strong evidences*; and **address a concern**, not *attack a concern*.

### Mini-check

Choose the natural collocation.

1. The report ___ attention to a serious gap.  
(A) draws (B) pulls

2. The committee needs to ___ a decision.  
(A) make (B) do

3. The new rule may ___ an impact on commuters.  
(A) have (B) make

4. The researchers ___ evidence from interviews.  
(A) gathered (B) caught

**Answer key:** 1-A; 2-A; 3-A; 4-A.

**Feedback:** The target combinations are *draw attention to*, *make a decision*, *have an impact on*, and *gather evidence*.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Reading  
**Instruction:** Complete the summary with the correct collocations.

The project team ___ evidence from residents, ___ a concern about access, and eventually ___ an agreement with the transport office.

(A) gathered / raised / reached  
(B) made / did / drew  
(C) caught / solved / provided  
(D) gave / took / had

**Model answer:** A.

**Feedback criteria:** The answer should demonstrate recognition of three complete collocations, not just individual verb meanings.

## B2 Material 6 — Word formation in context

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Vocabulary  
**Topic:** Parts of speech and derivation  
**Prerequisite:** Basic nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs  
**Suggested time:** 14 minutes  
**Tags:** word formation, prefixes, suffixes, word families

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to identify the required part of speech and form common academic and workplace words accurately.

### Explanation

Use the surrounding words to decide what form is needed. Articles and possessives often come before nouns: *an improvement*, *the organization’s success*. After linking verbs such as *be*, use an adjective: *The solution is practical.* After a main verb, an adverb may describe how an action occurs: *The team responded quickly.*

Common suffixes include **-tion**, **-ment**, and **-ness** for nouns; **-ive**, **-al**, and **-able** for adjectives; and **-ly** for adverbs. Prefixes can change meaning: **un-** means not, **re-** means again, and **over-** can mean too much.

Spelling may change: *decide → decision*, *rely → reliable*, *possible → impossible*.

### Examples

- The project’s **development** took six months.
- The method is highly **effective**.
- Participants responded **positively**.
- The revised plan is more **reliable**.

### Common mistake

Do not choose a word from the correct family without checking its grammatical role. In *The system works ___*, use **reliably**, not *reliable*. In *The system is ___*, use **reliable**.

### Mini-check

Complete the sentences with the correct form.

1. The training improved employee ___ (CONFIDENT).
2. The instructions were clear and ___ (PRACTICE).
3. The team responded ___ (QUICK).
4. The policy needs further ___ (DEVELOP).

**Answer key:** confidence; practical; quickly; development.

**Feedback:** The first and fourth blanks require nouns, the second an adjective, and the third an adverb.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Reading  
**Instruction:** Complete the paragraph with the correct forms.

The ___ (INTRODUCE) of the new service was successful because the staff received ___ (ADEQUATE) training and answered customers’ questions ___. (PATIENT)

**Model answer:** introduction; adequate; patiently.

**Feedback criteria:** Use sentence position and meaning together. The answer should contain a noun, an adjective, and an adverb.

## B2 Material 7 — Reading: identifying attitude and purpose

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Reading  
**Topic:** Author attitude and communicative purpose  
**Prerequisite:** Main idea and detail questions  
**Suggested time:** 15 minutes  
**Tags:** reading, attitude, purpose, tone, inference

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to distinguish what a writer says from how the writer feels and why the text was written.

### Explanation

A writer’s **purpose** is the reason for writing: to inform, recommend, criticize, compare, explain, or persuade. The writer’s **attitude** is the position or feeling expressed toward the topic, such as supportive, doubtful, critical, cautious, or enthusiastic.

Look for evaluative words and phrases. *A useful improvement* suggests approval. *A questionable solution* suggests doubt. *Although the plan has clear benefits, its costs remain uncertain* suggests a cautious or balanced attitude.

Do not confuse the topic with the purpose. A text about a new transport plan may be written to evaluate it, not merely to describe it. Also, do not choose an extreme attitude unless the writer uses clearly strong language.

### Examples

**Text:** “The program has reduced waiting times, although the limited schedule still prevents some residents from using it.”

- Purpose: to evaluate the program.
- Attitude: cautiously positive or balanced.
- Evidence: a benefit is recognized, but a limitation is also mentioned.

### Common mistake

Do not assume that mentioning a problem means the writer is completely negative. Balanced texts often recognize advantages and disadvantages.

### Mini-check

Read the sentence and choose the writer’s attitude.

“While the proposal could improve access, the committee should clarify how the additional staff would be funded.”

(A) Completely enthusiastic  
(B) Cautiously supportive  
(C) Uninterested  
(D) Strongly hostile

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** The writer recognizes a possible benefit but asks for clarification about a concern.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Reading  
**Instruction:** Read the passage and answer the questions.

The redesigned park is a welcome improvement, particularly for families who previously had few shaded areas to use. However, the city has not yet explained how the gardens will be maintained during the summer. Without a clear maintenance plan, the project may not deliver all of its promised benefits.

1. What is the writer’s main purpose?  
(A) To describe the park’s history. (B) To evaluate a redesign. (C) To advertise gardening jobs. (D) To explain summer weather.

2. What is the writer’s attitude?  
(A) Balanced but concerned. (B) Completely negative. (C) Indifferent. (D) Humorous.

**Model answer:** 1-B; 2-A.

**Feedback criteria:** Evidence includes praise for shaded areas and concern about maintenance. The answer should integrate both parts.

## B2 Material 8 — Reading: comparing two texts

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Reading  
**Topic:** Integrating information across sources  
**Prerequisite:** Main idea, detail, and inference strategies  
**Suggested time:** 16 minutes  
**Tags:** reading, multiple texts, comparison, synthesis

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to identify agreement, disagreement, and complementary information across two short texts.

### Explanation

When reading two texts, first identify the main claim of each one. Then compare them using categories such as benefits, concerns, causes, or recommendations. The correct answer may not be stated word for word in either text; it may summarize a relationship between them.

Create a small mental chart:

| Question | Text A | Text B |
|---|---|---|
| Main point | What does A emphasize? | What does B emphasize? |
| Position | Support, oppose, or qualify? | Support, oppose, or qualify? |
| Shared idea | What can both texts support? | What differs? |

Do not assume that two texts disagree simply because they focus on different aspects. One may describe a benefit while the other identifies a condition needed for that benefit to work.

### Examples

Text A: “The evening bus reduced travel difficulties for workers.”  
Text B: “The service is useful, but its limited frequency makes transfers difficult.”

Shared idea: The service offers a benefit, but its design still has limitations.

### Common mistake

Do not answer a multiple-text question using evidence from only one source when the question asks about both.

### Mini-check

Text A says a school garden provides fresh vegetables. Text B says students learn responsibility by caring for it. Which statement is supported by both?

(A) The garden is used only for science classes.  
(B) The garden offers practical benefits beyond decoration.  
(C) Students dislike outdoor activities.  
(D) The garden is too expensive to maintain.

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** Both texts present a practical benefit, although one focuses on food and the other on learning.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Reading  
**Instruction:** Read the two texts and choose the best integrated conclusion.

**Text A:** The university’s bike-share program has made short trips across campus faster and reduced the need for students to bring cars.

**Text B:** Some students appreciate the bike-share program but report that bicycles are difficult to find near the residence halls at peak times.

What conclusion is supported by both texts?

(A) The program has no effect on transportation.  
(B) The program is useful, but availability needs improvement.  
(C) Students prefer driving to cycling.  
(D) Residence halls should be moved closer to classrooms.

**Model answer:** B.

**Feedback criteria:** The answer must combine the benefit in Text A with the limitation in Text B.

## B2 Material 9 — Listening: intention, attitude, and implied meaning

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Listening  
**Topic:** Understanding what speakers mean indirectly  
**Prerequisite:** Listening for explicit details  
**Suggested time:** 15 minutes  
**Tags:** listening, intention, attitude, inference, indirect meaning

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to infer a speaker’s intention or attitude when the meaning is not stated directly.

### Explanation

Speakers often communicate indirectly. A phrase such as *That’s one way of looking at it* may indicate polite disagreement. *I’m not sure that will work* may express doubt rather than simple uncertainty. Listen to the relationship between words, tone, and context.

Pay attention to contrast markers, hesitation, and softening phrases: **actually**, **well**, **I see your point, but...**, **perhaps**, and **I’m afraid...** These expressions can signal a correction, reservation, or refusal.

To answer an intention question, ask: What does the speaker want the listener to understand or do? To answer an attitude question, ask: Does the speaker sound enthusiastic, doubtful, annoyed, relieved, or neutral?

### Examples

**Speaker A:** “The room is attractive, but it may be too small for fifty people.”  
Attitude: partly positive but concerned.  
Intention: to warn the group to reconsider the location.

### Common mistake

Do not focus on one positive or negative word. Interpret the whole exchange. “That sounds interesting, although I’m not convinced it is practical” is not strong approval.

### Mini-check

**Roteiro:**

**Man:** We could publish the report tomorrow.  
**Woman:** We could, but the final figures have not been checked yet.

What does the woman mean?

(A) The report is ready to publish immediately.  
(B) She thinks the report needs another review.  
(C) She does not know who wrote the report.  
(D) She wants to cancel the project.

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** The contrast “but” introduces a concern about unchecked figures.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Listening  
**Instruction:** Listen to the dialogue and identify the speaker’s attitude.

**Script for recording:**

**Woman:** Did you enjoy the new restaurant?  
**Man:** The food was excellent. I just wish the service had been a little faster.

How does the man feel about the restaurant?

(A) Completely dissatisfied.  
(B) Generally positive but mildly critical.  
(C) Unable to remember the visit.  
(D) Excited to work there.

**Model answer:** B.

**Feedback criteria:** The response should recognize praise for the food and a limited criticism of service.

## B2 Material 10 — Listening: note-taking and organization

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Listening  
**Topic:** Recording key information efficiently  
**Prerequisite:** Listening for main ideas and details  
**Suggested time:** 15 minutes  
**Tags:** listening, note-taking, organization, lectures

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to take brief notes that capture a talk’s main point, supporting details, examples, and next steps.

### Explanation

Effective notes are not a complete transcript. Use short phrases, symbols, and headings. Listen for signposts such as **first**, **in contrast**, **for example**, **as a result**, and **finally**. These words show the structure of the talk.

Before listening, create categories based on the task. If the question asks for a problem, a cause, and a solution, leave space for those three items. Write key nouns and verbs rather than every grammatical word.

After listening, check whether your notes show relationships. An isolated word such as “cost” is less useful than “high cost → fewer participants”.

### Examples

**Talk structure:**

- Problem: low attendance
- Cause: inconvenient schedule
- Change: evening sessions
- Expected result: more working adults can attend

### Common mistake

Do not write so much that you stop listening. Abbreviations such as *govt.*, *esp.*, and arrows can help, but use symbols consistently.

### Mini-check

**Script:** The museum had low attendance on weekday mornings. After surveying visitors, the staff learned that many local workers could not arrive before noon. The museum will therefore open later twice a week and monitor attendance for three months.

Complete the notes:

- Problem: ___
- Cause: ___
- Change: ___
- Period of monitoring: ___

**Answer key:** low weekday-morning attendance; local workers could not arrive before noon; open later twice a week; three months.

**Feedback:** Strong notes capture the problem, the reason, the response, and the time period without copying the entire script.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Listening  
**Instruction:** Listen to the script and complete the four-column note frame.

**Script for recording:**

The language center is introducing a conversation exchange. Students will meet in pairs for thirty minutes each week. The first ten minutes will be in English, and the next ten will be in another language; the final ten minutes will be used for feedback. Students must register by Monday because places are limited.

| Purpose | Details |
|---|---|
| Activity | ___ |
| Weekly length | ___ |
| Organization | ___ |
| Registration | ___ |

**Model answer:** Activity: conversation exchange; weekly length: thirty minutes; organization: ten minutes in English, ten in another language, ten for feedback; registration: by Monday, because places are limited.

**Feedback criteria:** Notes should preserve the sequence and the registration deadline. Accept phrases rather than full sentences.

## B2 Material 11 — Writing: developing a balanced opinion

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Writing  
**Topic:** Position, reasons, examples, and counterpoints  
**Prerequisite:** Paragraph organization  
**Suggested time:** 18 minutes  
**Tags:** writing, opinion, argument, counterargument, cohesion

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to write a clear opinion response that includes a position, developed reasons, an example, and a response to a counterargument.

### Explanation

A balanced opinion response does not need to give equal space to every view, but it should show that the writer understands a possible alternative. Begin with a clear position. Develop each reason by explaining why it matters and adding an example or consequence.

Useful organization:

1. **Position:** I believe that...
2. **Reason:** One important reason is...
3. **Development:** This matters because...
4. **Example:** For instance...
5. **Counterpoint:** Although some people argue that...
6. **Response:** this concern can be addressed by...

Use connectors accurately: **however**, **although**, **therefore**, **as a result**, **in addition**, and **for example**.

### Examples

Weak: “Public transport is good because it is good for people.”  
Stronger: “Public transport can reduce commuting costs, which is especially helpful for students and workers on limited budgets.”

### Common mistake

Do not list reasons without development. Two explained reasons are usually more effective than four unsupported claims.

### Mini-check

Which sentence best develops the reason “flexible schedules can help employees”?

(A) Flexible schedules are good.  
(B) They can allow employees to arrange work around childcare or long commutes.  
(C) Employees have schedules.  
(D) This topic is important.

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** B explains a practical mechanism and gives two relevant examples of who may benefit.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Write 150–180 words answering: **Should universities offer more courses in the evening?** State your position, give two developed reasons, include one example, and respond to one concern.

**Model response:** A strong response clearly answers the question, develops access and scheduling or another relevant reason, gives a concrete example, and acknowledges a concern such as staff availability.

**Feedback criteria:** Check task completion, organization, development, grammatical control, lexical range, and clarity. The next improvement should be specific, such as adding a consequence to the second reason.

## B2 Material 12 — Speaking: building a developed response

**Level:** B2  
**Primary skill:** Speaking  
**Topic:** Extended opinion and comparison  
**Prerequisite:** Short answers with reasons  
**Suggested time:** 45 seconds preparation + 90 seconds speaking  
**Tags:** speaking, opinion, comparison, fluency, coherence

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to deliver a structured spoken response with a clear position, connected reasons, an example, and a conclusion.

### Explanation

For a longer response, use a simple four-part structure: answer the question, explain the first reason, add a second reason or comparison, and conclude. You do not need to memorize a speech. Use signposts to guide the listener: **first**, **another point is**, **for example**, **however**, and **overall**.

When comparing options, use **whereas**, **while**, **more convenient than**, and **the main advantage is**. When expressing an opinion, use **I would argue that**, **in my view**, or **I tend to think that**.

If you need time, pause briefly and use a natural planning phrase. Avoid long silence, but do not fill every pause with repeated words.

### Examples

**Prompt:** Is it better to study alone or with other people?

**Model opening:** “I would say that studying with other people is more useful for discussion-based subjects, whereas studying alone may be better when concentration is important.”

### Common mistake

Do not answer the question with a list of unrelated personal details. Every example should support the main position.

### Mini-check

Which opening gives the clearest answer?

(A) “There are many things to say.”  
(B) “In my view, working in a small group is more effective because members can exchange ideas and notice problems that one person might miss.”  
(C) “Groups, people, work, school.”  
(D) “I don’t know, maybe yes.”

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** B gives a direct position and previews two connected reasons.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Speaking  
**Instruction:** Speak for 90 seconds: **Which is more useful for language learning: watching films or reading books?** Compare the options, choose one, give two reasons, and include an example.

**Model response:** “Both activities can expand vocabulary, but I would choose reading books because readers can slow down and examine unfamiliar expressions. Films are useful for pronunciation and natural speed; however, a learner may miss details. For example, I can underline phrases in a book and review them later. Overall, reading gives me more control over the learning process.”

**Feedback criteria:** Evaluate development, coherence, communicative appropriacy, and language control. If only a transcript is available, do not make firm claims about pronunciation, rhythm, or delivery.

## B2+ Material 13 — Hedging and degrees of certainty

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Grammar  
**Topic:** Making claims precise and appropriately cautious  
**Prerequisite:** B2 modals and conditionals  
**Suggested time:** 15 minutes  
**Tags:** hedging, modality, academic register, certainty

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to qualify claims so that their level of certainty matches the available evidence.

### Explanation

**Hedging** means using language that avoids presenting an interpretation as an unquestionable fact. It is useful when evidence is limited, when discussing predictions, or when acknowledging variation.

Common hedging devices include **may**, **might**, **appears to**, **tends to**, **is likely to**, **could suggest**, **in some cases**, and **there is evidence that**. Compare:

- Strong: “The new schedule improves productivity.”
- More precise: “The new schedule **appears to improve** productivity among some teams.”

Hedging does not mean being vague. A strong academic or professional claim identifies its scope and evidence. Avoid excessive hedging when the evidence is clear, but avoid absolute words such as **always**, **never**, and **proves** unless they are genuinely justified.

### Examples

- The findings **suggest that** short reviews improve recall.
- The policy **is likely to affect** commuters differently.
- This result **may be partly explained by** the small sample.

### Common mistake

Do not combine too many weak expressions: *It may perhaps possibly suggest that...* Choose one or two precise devices.

### Mini-check

Choose the most appropriately cautious sentence.

(A) The survey proves that everyone prefers online learning.  
(B) The survey may suggest that many participants prefer online learning.  
(C) The survey definitely perhaps shows a preference.  
(D) The survey never indicates a preference.

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** B limits the claim to what a survey may suggest and avoids unsupported universals.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Rewrite the claim to make it appropriately cautious: **“Working from home makes all employees more productive.”**

**Model answer:** “Working from home may increase productivity for some employees, particularly when they have a quiet workspace and clear expectations.”

**Feedback criteria:** The revision should reduce the universal claim, identify a possible scope, and preserve a clear meaning.

## B2+ Material 14 — Emphasis and information structure

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Grammar  
**Topic:** Highlighting important information  
**Prerequisite:** Complex sentences and auxiliary verbs  
**Suggested time:** 15 minutes  
**Tags:** emphasis, cleft sentences, inversion, information structure

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to use emphasis structures to focus the reader’s or listener’s attention on a particular part of a message.

### Explanation

English can emphasize information through **cleft sentences**: *What the project needs is clearer communication.* This structure presents the important information after *is*. Another pattern is **It was...that/who...**: *It was the lack of funding that delayed the project.*

In formal or literary contexts, negative or limiting expressions can trigger inversion: *Only after the meeting did we understand the problem.* This is more marked than the neutral form: *We understood the problem only after the meeting.*

Use emphasis purposefully. It can contrast two ideas, correct an assumption, or highlight a cause. Do not use it in every sentence.

### Examples

- **What matters most is** how the policy affects users.
- **It was the final paragraph that** changed my opinion.
- **Only later did** the team recognize the risk.

### Common mistake

After expressions such as *only later* at the beginning, use inversion: **did the team recognize**, not *the team recognized* in the marked formal pattern.

### Mini-check

Choose the correct sentence.

1. Only after the trial ___ the researchers identify the flaw.  
(A) did (B) the researchers did

2. What the proposal needs ___ a clearer timeline.  
(A) is (B) are

3. It was the transport delay ___ caused the problem.  
(A) who (B) that

**Answer key:** 1-A; 2-A; 3-B.

**Feedback:** Inversion follows the fronted limiting expression. The singular complement “a clearer timeline” determines “is,” and “that” refers to the cause.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Rewrite the sentence to emphasize the cause: **“Poor communication caused the delay.”** Use **It was...that...**

**Model answer:** “It was poor communication that caused the delay.”

**Feedback criteria:** The response must place the emphasized information after *was* and maintain the original meaning.

## B2+ Material 15 — Participle clauses and concise writing

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Grammar  
**Topic:** Reducing repeated subjects and clauses  
**Prerequisite:** Participles and clause relationships  
**Suggested time:** 15 minutes  
**Tags:** participle clauses, concise writing, cause, time, contrast

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to use participle clauses to make connected writing more concise without creating unclear subjects.

### Explanation

A present participle clause with **-ing** can show an action happening at the same time or a cause: *Knowing that the road was closed, the driver chose another route.* A past participle clause can show a passive meaning: *Designed for small groups, the room contains movable tables.*

The implied subject of the participle clause should normally be the subject of the main clause. Compare the clear sentence *Walking through the park, Maya noticed the new sign* with the unclear *Walking through the park, the new sign attracted Maya.* The sign cannot walk.

Participle clauses are useful in formal writing, but do not use them when a full clause would be clearer.

### Examples

- **Having reviewed** the data, the team changed its recommendation.
- **Built to reduce noise**, the panels are useful in shared offices.
- **Although concerned about cost**, the committee approved the plan.

### Common mistake

Do not create a dangling modifier by giving the introductory phrase the wrong subject.

### Mini-check

Choose the sentence with a logical subject.

(A) Having completed the survey, the results were analyzed.  
(B) Having completed the survey, the researchers analyzed the results.

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** The researchers completed the survey; the results did not complete it.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Combine the sentences using a participle clause: **The team reviewed the evidence. It decided to extend the pilot program.**

**Model answer:** “Having reviewed the evidence, the team decided to extend the pilot program.”

**Feedback criteria:** Check that the team is the implied subject of both actions and that the sequence is clear.

## B2+ Material 16 — Nominalization and register

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Grammar  
**Topic:** Managing formal written style  
**Prerequisite:** Word formation and clause structure  
**Suggested time:** 15 minutes  
**Tags:** nominalization, formal register, academic writing

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to recognize and use nominalization while maintaining clarity and avoiding unnecessarily dense sentences.

### Explanation

**Nominalization** changes a verb or adjective into a noun, often making writing more formal: *decide → decision*, *improve → improvement*, *available → availability*. It can help connect ideas in reports: *The committee decided to expand the service* → *The committee’s decision to expand the service...*

However, too many abstract nouns can make writing heavy. Compare:

- Direct: “The team reviewed the plan and changed the schedule.”
- Dense: “The team’s review of the plan resulted in a schedule modification.”

The second may suit a formal report, but the first is often clearer. Use nominalization when it helps organize information, not simply to sound sophisticated.

### Examples

- The policy was implemented after careful **consideration**.
- The **expansion** of the program created new opportunities.
- The committee **analyzed** the data before making a recommendation.

### Common mistake

Do not replace every verb with a noun. Excessive nominalization can hide who performed the action.

### Mini-check

Which sentence is clearer and appropriately formal?

(A) The implementation of the review was undertaken by the team.  
(B) The team implemented the review.

**Answer key:** B, unless the agent is intentionally unimportant.

**Feedback:** The direct version identifies the actor and action clearly. Formality should not reduce clarity.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Rewrite the sentence using one useful nominalization, but keep the actor clear: **The committee decided to revise the policy after it considered the feedback.**

**Model answer:** “The committee’s decision to revise the policy followed its consideration of the feedback.”

**Feedback criteria:** The answer should use nominalization accurately while preserving the committee as the responsible actor.

## B2+ Material 17 — Register: formal, neutral, and conversational language

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Vocabulary  
**Topic:** Choosing language for audience and purpose  
**Prerequisite:** Common professional vocabulary  
**Suggested time:** 14 minutes  
**Tags:** register, formal English, tone, audience

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to adjust vocabulary and expressions to suit formal, neutral, and conversational contexts.

### Explanation

Register is the level of formality appropriate for a situation. A message to a close friend can use contractions and conversational expressions. A message to an institution may require a more neutral or formal style.

| Conversational | Neutral or formal |
|---|---|
| find out | discover / determine |
| put off | postpone |
| a lot of | many / considerable |
| get in touch | contact |
| fix the problem | resolve the issue |
| I want to ask | I am writing to ask |

Formal language should not be unnecessarily complicated. A clear neutral expression is often better than an impressive but vague word.

### Examples

- Conversational: “Can you look into this?”
- Neutral: “Could you investigate this issue?”
- Formal: “I would appreciate it if you could investigate this matter.”

### Common mistake

Do not make a text formal by replacing every simple word with a longer one. *Utilize* is not automatically better than *use*, and *commence* is often less natural than *begin*.

### Mini-check

Choose the most appropriate sentence for a formal complaint.

(A) Your service was a mess. Fix it soon.  
(B) I am writing to request an explanation of the delay and information about the available remedies.  
(C) Hey, what happened with my order?

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** B is specific, respectful, and appropriate for an institutional recipient.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Rewrite this conversational message for a university administrator: **“I can’t make the meeting. Can we move it?”**

**Model answer:** “Unfortunately, I will be unable to attend the meeting. Would it be possible to reschedule it for another time?”

**Feedback criteria:** Preserve the request, add appropriate politeness, and avoid slang or overly casual wording.

## B2+ Material 18 — Precision vocabulary: connotation and strength

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Vocabulary  
**Topic:** Choosing words with the right force and implication  
**Prerequisite:** General academic and professional vocabulary  
**Suggested time:** 14 minutes  
**Tags:** connotation, lexical precision, intensity, synonyms

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to select vocabulary that expresses the intended degree of approval, criticism, certainty, or urgency.

### Explanation

Synonyms are rarely identical in strength or connotation. **Concerned**, **worried**, and **alarmed** all express unease, but *alarmed* is much stronger. **Interesting**, **useful**, and **valuable** express different kinds of positive evaluation. **Mistake**, **error**, and **failure** also differ in seriousness and context.

When choosing a word, ask three questions: How strong should the word be? Is the attitude positive, negative, or neutral? Is the word suitable for the audience?

| Less strong | More strong |
|---|---|
| concern | serious concern |
| problem | major obstacle |
| improve | transform |
| possible | highly likely |
| unusual | exceptional |

### Examples

- “The plan has a **minor limitation**” is less critical than “The plan has a **fundamental weakness**.”
- “The results are **promising**” is positive but not a guarantee.

### Common mistake

Do not choose a strong word simply because it sounds advanced. If the evidence shows a small delay, calling it a “disaster” is inaccurate.

### Mini-check

Which word best completes the sentence?

The evidence is ___, but further research is needed before a firm conclusion can be reached.

(A) conclusive (B) promising (C) impossible (D) irrelevant

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** “Promising” is positive but appropriately limited; “conclusive” would conflict with the need for further research.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Reading  
**Instruction:** Choose the best description of the writer’s evaluation.

“The pilot produced encouraging results, although the sample was too small to support a definitive claim.”

(A) The writer sees the findings as completely proven.  
(B) The writer sees the findings as positive but limited.  
(C) The writer rejects the pilot as useless.  
(D) The writer believes the sample was unusually large.

**Model answer:** B.

**Feedback criteria:** The answer must recognize both “encouraging” and the limitation created by the small sample.

## B2+ Material 19 — Reading: evidence, interpretation, and assumption

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Reading  
**Topic:** Distinguishing what a text states from what it suggests  
**Prerequisite:** Inference and attitude questions  
**Suggested time:** 16 minutes  
**Tags:** reading, evidence, interpretation, assumptions, critical reading

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to separate explicit evidence, reasonable interpretation, and unsupported assumption.

### Explanation

An **explicit statement** is directly expressed: *Attendance increased by 18 percent.* An **interpretation** is a conclusion that is reasonably supported: *The new schedule may have contributed to the increase.* An **assumption** goes beyond the available evidence: *Every student preferred the new schedule.*

When answering inference questions, choose the strongest conclusion that the text supports, not the most interesting conclusion. Look for limiting words such as **some**, **may**, **appears**, **according to**, and **in this sample**.

A useful test is to ask: “Which exact words support this answer?” If you cannot identify evidence, the option may be an unsupported assumption.

### Examples

Text: “Attendance rose after the center added evening sessions. The survey included 40 regular participants.”

- Explicit: Attendance rose.
- Reasonable interpretation: Evening sessions may have helped.
- Unsupported assumption: Every resident wanted evening sessions.

### Common mistake

Do not confuse sequence with proof of cause. If one event follows another, the text may suggest a connection, but it does not automatically prove causation.

### Mini-check

Text: “After the notice was redesigned, fewer visitors asked for directions. The center did not collect information about visitors who did not enter.”

Which conclusion is best supported?

(A) The redesigned notice may have improved clarity.  
(B) The notice solved every access problem.  
(C) All visitors read the notice.  
(D) The center no longer needs staff.

**Answer key:** A.

**Feedback:** A is a cautious interpretation supported by the sequence and the reduced questions. The other choices make claims the text cannot support.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Reading  
**Instruction:** Read the passage and classify each statement as Explicit, Supported interpretation, or Unsupported assumption.

The community center introduced online registration. In the first month, 62 percent of participants registered online. Staff members reported spending less time entering forms manually, but no survey measured whether participants found the new system easier.

1. More than half of participants used online registration.  
2. The new system may have reduced staff paperwork.  
3. Participants definitely preferred the new system.

**Model answer:** 1 Explicit; 2 Supported interpretation; 3 Unsupported assumption.

**Feedback criteria:** The learner should distinguish numerical evidence, a cautious conclusion from staff reports, and a claim that was not measured.

## B2+ Material 20 — Reading: synthesis across multiple sources

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Reading  
**Topic:** Combining claims and qualifications  
**Prerequisite:** Comparing two texts  
**Suggested time:** 18 minutes  
**Tags:** reading, synthesis, multiple sources, qualification

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to synthesize several short sources into one accurate conclusion without erasing important qualifications.

### Explanation

Synthesis means combining information to produce a more complete understanding. Begin by identifying the focus of each source. One source may present a benefit, another a limitation, and a third a condition for success.

When writing or selecting a synthesis, preserve the strength of each claim. If one source says a program **may** help and another says it **requires** additional staffing, do not summarize them as “the program is successful.” A better synthesis is: *The program may improve access, but its success depends on adequate staffing.*

Use reporting verbs such as **argues**, **suggests**, **notes**, and **emphasizes** when the source’s position matters.

### Examples

- Source A: flexible hours increase access.
- Source B: evening staffing is expensive.
- Synthesis: Flexible hours may increase access, although implementation requires additional staffing resources.

### Common mistake

Do not list each source separately without showing the relationship between them. Synthesis requires comparison, contrast, cause, or qualification.

### Mini-check

Source A says a digital archive makes materials easier to search. Source B says some older materials are difficult to scan. Which synthesis is best?

(A) Digital archives are always easy to create.  
(B) Digital archives can improve access, but technical limitations may affect what can be included.  
(C) Older materials are not valuable.  
(D) Searching is more difficult online.

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** B combines the benefit and limitation without exaggerating either source.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Write 80–100 words synthesizing the two notes.

- A: Short daily practice helps learners remember vocabulary.
- B: Learners also need opportunities to use new words in meaningful conversation.

**Model response:** “Short daily practice can support vocabulary retention, but memorization alone may not be sufficient. Learners also need meaningful opportunities to use new words in conversation. Therefore, an effective routine could combine brief review sessions with regular speaking activities.”

**Feedback criteria:** The response should integrate both sources, use an accurate relationship such as contrast or addition, and avoid claiming that one method is universally best.

## B2+ Material 21 — Listening: indirect disagreement and stance

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Listening  
**Topic:** Politeness, disagreement, and speaker position  
**Prerequisite:** B2 inference and attitude strategies  
**Suggested time:** 16 minutes  
**Tags:** listening, disagreement, stance, pragmatics, tone

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to identify agreement, reservation, and indirect disagreement in realistic conversations.

### Explanation

Speakers often soften disagreement to remain polite. Expressions such as **I see what you mean**, **That could be true**, **I’m not entirely convinced**, and **I wonder whether** may introduce a different view. The strongest clue is often what follows the polite opening.

Compare:

- “I see your point, and I agree.” — agreement.
- “I see your point, but I’m not sure it applies here.” — partial agreement or reservation.
- “That’s an interesting suggestion. I wonder whether we have the resources.” — polite concern.

Listen for the speaker’s final position rather than the first polite phrase. Also distinguish disagreement with an idea from criticism of a person.

### Examples

**Speaker A:** “We should cancel the training.”  
**Speaker B:** “I understand the budget concern, but perhaps we could reduce the number of sessions instead.”

Speaker B recognizes the concern but disagrees with cancellation.

### Common mistake

Do not interpret *I see your point* as complete agreement. It may simply acknowledge that the other person’s argument is understandable.

### Mini-check

**Script:** “The design is attractive. I’m just not convinced that users will find it easy to navigate.”

What is the speaker’s position?

(A) Strong approval of both design and usability.  
(B) Approval of appearance but concern about usability.  
(C) Rejection of the entire project.  
(D) No opinion.

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** The contrast between “attractive” and “not convinced” separates positive evaluation from concern.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Listening  
**Instruction:** Listen to the exchange and identify the recommendation implied by the second speaker.

**Script for recording:**

**Student A:** We could hold the presentation outdoors.  
**Student B:** That might be enjoyable, although we should probably have an indoor option in case the weather changes.

What does Student B recommend?

(A) Cancelling the presentation.  
(B) Moving it to another city.  
(C) Keeping an indoor alternative available.  
(D) Refusing to discuss the weather.

**Model answer:** C.

**Feedback criteria:** The learner should interpret “we should probably have” as a recommendation and connect it to the weather concern.

## B2+ Material 22 — Listening: discourse markers and changing direction

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Listening  
**Topic:** Following complex spoken organization  
**Prerequisite:** Note-taking and main-idea listening  
**Suggested time:** 16 minutes  
**Tags:** listening, discourse markers, organization, contrast, correction

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to follow a speaker’s organization when the speaker adds, corrects, contrasts, or narrows information.

### Explanation

Discourse markers show how a speaker is organizing ideas. **To begin with** introduces a first point. **That said**, **however**, and **nevertheless** signal contrast. **More specifically** narrows a general claim. **In other words** introduces a restatement. **Actually**, **rather**, and **what I mean is** may correct or clarify an earlier statement.

Do not treat every sentence as equally important. A speaker may first mention a general plan and then revise it: *We expected to open in May—actually, early June is more realistic.* The final version is the current information.

### Examples

- “The service is inexpensive. **That said**, it is not available in every neighborhood.”
- “The results were positive. **More specifically**, attendance increased among first-time visitors.”
- “The event begins at five—**rather**, it begins at five-thirty.”

### Common mistake

Do not write down an earlier number or date after the speaker corrects it. Mark corrections with an arrow or a star.

### Mini-check

**Script:** “The workshop was originally planned for Room 3. Actually, because of the larger registration, it will take place in the auditorium.”

Where will the workshop take place?

(A) Room 2 (B) Room 3 (C) The auditorium (D) Online

**Answer key:** C.

**Feedback:** “Actually” introduces a correction to the initial location.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Listening  
**Instruction:** Listen and complete the final version of the notes.

**Script for recording:**

The project will first focus on local schools. That said, the team is not limiting the program to children. More specifically, after the pilot phase, it hopes to offer workshops for parents as well.

**Notes:**

- Initial focus: ___
- Later expansion: ___

**Model answer:** Initial focus: local schools; later expansion: workshops for parents.

**Feedback criteria:** The learner should identify both the initial scope and the later, more specific expansion.

## B2+ Material 23 — Writing: responding to a counterargument

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Writing  
**Topic:** Concession, rebuttal, and persuasive balance  
**Prerequisite:** B2 opinion writing  
**Suggested time:** 20 minutes  
**Tags:** writing, counterargument, rebuttal, concession, persuasion

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to acknowledge a reasonable objection and respond to it without weakening their own position.

### Explanation

A counterargument demonstrates that you understand a different perspective. A rebuttal explains why your position remains convincing or describes a condition that addresses the concern.

Useful patterns include:

- **Although** the proposal would require additional funding, it could reduce long-term costs.
- **It is true that** online meetings can feel less personal. **However**, regular interactive activities can address this limitation.
- **Some people may argue that** the change is inconvenient. **Nevertheless**, the current system creates a greater problem for users who...

The counterargument should be relevant and plausible. Do not invent an extreme position merely to dismiss it easily.

### Examples

Weak: “Some people disagree, but they are wrong.”  
Stronger: “Although printed materials are familiar to many users, a digital option would improve access for people who cannot visit the center in person.”

### Common mistake

Do not present a counterargument and stop. The reader needs to know how it affects your final position.

### Mini-check

Which sentence contains both concession and response?

(A) Online courses are flexible.  
(B) Some learners prefer classrooms.  
(C) Although online courses may feel less personal, regular live sessions can provide meaningful interaction.  
(D) Classrooms have chairs.

**Answer key:** C.

**Feedback:** C acknowledges a limitation and presents a way to address it.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Writing  
**Instruction:** Write 170–200 words answering: **Should public libraries reduce printed collections to create more digital services?** Include a clear position, one counterargument, one rebuttal, and a specific example.

**Model response:** A strong response may support a mixed collection: digital services can expand access, while printed books remain important for users with limited technology access. The rebuttal should propose a balanced transition rather than ignoring the concern.

**Feedback criteria:** Assess argument clarity, relevance of the counterargument, effectiveness of the rebuttal, cohesion, grammatical control, lexical precision, and audience awareness.

## B2+ Material 24 — Speaking: reformulation, negotiation, and persuasive nuance

**Level:** B2+  
**Primary skill:** Speaking  
**Topic:** Managing interaction and adapting language  
**Prerequisite:** B2 extended speaking  
**Suggested time:** 60 seconds preparation + 120 seconds speaking  
**Tags:** speaking, reformulation, negotiation, persuasion, nuance

> **Original MET-style practice — created for learning; not an official MET item.**

### Objective

By the end of this material, learners will be able to present a nuanced position, respond to a concern, negotiate a practical solution, and reformulate when a word or idea is difficult to express.

### Explanation

Advanced speaking is not only about using complex vocabulary. It also involves managing the conversation. If you cannot remember a word, describe it: **It is a tool that...**, **What I mean is...**, or **It is similar to...** This keeps communication moving.

To negotiate, combine a reason with flexibility: **I would prefer X because..., although I understand that... Perhaps we could...** To persuade, connect the proposal to the listener’s priorities. Use cautious language when appropriate: **This could**, **It would probably**, or **One possible advantage is**.

A well-developed response often follows this structure: position, reason, qualification, response to concern, and practical recommendation.

### Examples

**Situation:** A student group must choose between a large event and several small events.

**Model response:** “I would lean toward several smaller events because they may be easier to organize and more inclusive. That said, a large event could attract more attention. Perhaps the group could begin with two small events and evaluate participation before planning a larger one.”

### Common mistake

Do not confuse nuance with indecision. You can acknowledge a limitation and still make a clear recommendation.

### Mini-check

Which response is both clear and nuanced?

(A) “Everything is good. I cannot decide.”  
(B) “I recommend the smaller event because it is easier to manage, although a larger event might attract more publicity. We could test the format first.”  
(C) “The event is eventful.”  
(D) “No option works.”

**Answer key:** B.

**Feedback:** B gives a recommendation, acknowledges an alternative benefit, and proposes a practical next step.

### Use it in the MET-style task

**Skill:** Speaking  
**Instruction:** You are advising a college committee. The committee has funding for either a weekly tutoring program or one large study day. Speak for two minutes. Choose one option, acknowledge a disadvantage, suggest a way to manage it, and conclude with a recommendation.

**Model response:** “I would recommend the weekly tutoring program because it offers support throughout the term rather than on a single day. The main disadvantage is that attendance may be uneven. However, the committee could use online registration and rotate the schedule to reach more students. A large study day might create excitement, but it would not provide continuing support. For these reasons, I would invest in the weekly program and review attendance after the first month.”

**Feedback criteria:** Evaluate development, coherence, communicative appropriacy, control of complex language, response to a disadvantage, and practicality of the recommendation. If only a transcript is available, pronunciation, rhythm, and delivery cannot be fully evaluated.

## Implementation note

Each material can be displayed in four stages: **Learn**, with the explanation; **Check**, with the mini-check; **Apply**, with the original MET-style task; and **Review**, with the answer key, rationale, and next action. For Listening materials, keep the recording script hidden during the first attempt and reveal it during review. For Writing and Speaking materials, treat the model as one possible response rather than a single required answer.
